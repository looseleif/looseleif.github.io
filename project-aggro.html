Master's research / CHOICE Robotics Lab

Robotic Search in Clutter

Self-supervised mechanical search, scalable experimentation, and robotic manipulation.

September 2022 - July 2024

My master's research at the University of Minnesota investigated how robots can uncover and retrieve objects hidden in clutter. I developed the physical platform, perception pipeline, and reinforcement-learning experiments needed to study mechanical search across changing environments.

The work expanded experimentation through automatically generated clutter, task-specific reward functions, and six policy variants trained for 3,000 iterations each. It connects target discovery, uncovering, and singulation with a platform designed to support larger-scale experimentation and future real-world online learning.

My contribution spans robot integration, camera geometry, self-supervised Q-learning, and evaluation in simulation with initial physical validation. The central question is how a robot should change its surroundings to make a hidden target visible and accessible.

Master's thesis

My thesis brings together an instrumented robotic workspace, repeatable visual observations, and learned policies for searching through clutter. I studied how reward design and target information change the way a robot uncovers an object, then connected those findings to a three-stage retrieval process: reveal, uncover, and isolate for grasping.

M.S. in Electrical and Computer Engineering / July 2024

The research problem

A camera cannot identify every object in a pile from a single view. A robot sometimes has to move the surrounding objects to learn what is underneath. My thesis treats that physical interaction as part of perception: each push changes both the scene and the information available for the next decision.

I studied two especially difficult conditions: extreme occlusion, where only a small part of the target is visible, and full occlusion, where there is no visible target mask. These conditions require different ways to guide exploration and measure progress. The work examines how to uncover a target while limiting unnecessary disruption to the surrounding clutter.

A platform for repeatable experiments

I integrated a FarmBot Genesis Cartesian robot with RGB-D perception and an adapted gripper to run mechanical-search experiments in a configurable workspace. The hardware work included motion and power constraints, end-effector integration, camera placement, and translating image coordinates into physical actions.

A major part of the work was making the visual input consistent. An overhead Intel RealSense camera reduced the geometric inconsistency and recalibration burden encountered with an angled external camera. I examined projection and deprojection, cropping, depth information, and segmentation so the learned action maps corresponded more reliably to the workspace.

This platform was designed around repeated experimentation. Stable camera geometry and repeatable observations are essential when the same robot must collect data, execute an action, observe its effect, and continue learning over many interactions.

Training across changing clutter

I extended the simulation experiments with automatic generation of structured clutter. Instead of manually rebuilding each test scene, the system places predefined clutter arrangements at varied positions and rotations. This makes it possible to repeatedly test hidden-target scenarios while varying their geometry.

The learning loop connects an observation to an action, measures the change in target visibility, and uses that feedback to update the policy through deep Q-learning and experience replay. For the uncovering experiments, I focused training on the pushing network, allowing the reward design to concentrate on making the target more visible.

The thesis evaluates six policy variants with 3,000 training iterations per policy. The variants explore baseline visibility rewards, penalty and strict reward schemes, pseudo-target guidance, fine-tuning an existing policy, and combining fine-tuning with pseudo-target guidance. This provides a controlled way to examine which parts of the learning setup change the resulting behavior.

Learning to uncover a hidden target

For a partially visible target, the baseline reward uses the change in the visible target mask to reinforce useful uncovering actions. This gives the robot feedback about whether a push exposes more of the object. The strict and penalty variants investigate how different reward rules affect that behavior.

For a fully hidden target, I investigated a pseudo-target mask: a region indicating where to search even when the object itself is not visible. This supplies spatial guidance for the first uncovering actions. The physical feasibility tests used manually placed pseudo-target regions.

The resulting retrieval strategy separates three jobs. A guided policy begins revealing a fully occluded target; the baseline uncovering policy then exposes more of it; and the established grasping pipeline handles singulation, creating room around the object for retrieval. This separation makes the purpose of each stage explicit and gives each policy a task-specific learning signal.

Evaluation and findings

After training, I evaluated uncovering efficiency over 100 iterations per policy using generated, stylized clutter. I compared learning stability and observed behavior alongside the task results. The baseline policy showed stable convergence, and both the baseline and pseudo-target policies learned useful uncovering behavior in the evaluated scenarios.

The experiments exposed a practical tradeoff: a policy can move quickly through clutter while also disturbing more of the surrounding scene. Fine-tuned policies retained some of that disruptive behavior, while reward changes did not consistently improve performance. These observations motivated treating target discovery, uncovering, and singulation as distinct stages.

The results are bounded by the structured clutter used for training and evaluation. Performance degraded when those arrangements became highly disordered. Physical tests of the baseline and pseudo-target policies provided initial feasibility evidence; broader real-world validation remains a next step.

Toward larger-scale online learning

The longer-term goal is a robotic system that can run repeated search experiments and learn from its own interactions with less manual setup. The hardware, overhead perception, and automatic clutter generation address different parts of that goal: consistent measurements, repeatable actions, and varied environments for policy development.

The thesis establishes simulation-based training and initial physical validation as the completed experimental work. Sustained real-world online training is the next research direction enabled by that foundation. Extending it would involve autonomous scene resets, longer hardware runs, and evaluation across a wider range of objects and clutter structures.

References & further reading

The papers below provide the prior methods and simulation resources behind this research. My platform, training experiments, and findings are documented in my master's thesis, particularly Chapters 2 through 5.

A Deep Learning Approach to Grasping the Invisible

Yang Yang, Hengyue Liang, and Changhyun Choi. IEEE Robotics and Automation Letters, 2020.

The target-directed search and manipulation framework underlying the thesis, including exploration, push/grasp coordination, and self-supervised learning.

IOSG: Image-driven Object Searching and Grasping

Houjian Yu, Xibai Lou, Yang Yang, and Changhyun Choi. IROS, 2023.

Related CHOICE Robotics Lab work on searching for a target from a reference image, with hierarchical policies for exploration and retrieval.

Learning Synergies between Pushing and Grasping with Self-supervised Deep Reinforcement Learning

Andy Zeng, Shuran Song, Stefan Welker, Johnny Lee, Alberto Rodriguez, and Thomas Funkhouser. Princeton University, Google, and MIT. IROS, 2018.

The visual pushing-and-grasping work connecting pixel-wise action predictions with self-supervised Q-learning. Its released implementation includes the V-REP environment, now known as CoppeliaSim, for training and evaluating manipulation policies.
